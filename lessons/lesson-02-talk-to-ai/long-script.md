# Lesson 2 — Talk to AI

**Version:** Long form  
**Runtime:** 13–16 minutes (includes on-screen demo)  
**On-screen title:** Talk to AI  
**Fridge line:** One tool. One real question. One useful answer.

**Objective:** The learner opens one AI tool, asks one real question from their life, judges whether the answer is useful, and asks one follow-up — without pasting anything private.

**Tone:** Calm coach. Show the screen. Celebrate a boring win.

## What this tool is — in one breath

People say “AI” as if it were a person living in the computer.

It is not a person. It does not know you. It does not care about you. It is a very fast pattern machine that has read a large amount of text and can write a reply that *sounds* sure of itself.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
AI in this lesson  
A tool that reads your words  
and writes a useful reply  
You stay in charge

That last line is the whole course. You stay in charge.

It can draft a letter, turn leftovers into a menu, explain a dense paragraph, or give you practice interview questions. It can also be **confidently wrong**. It can invent a citation, a phone number, or a medical certainty. We will treat it like a sharp intern: helpful, fast, and never the final word on your health, your money, or the law.

`[PAUSE]`

AARP asked adults our age what they want from this technology. They want everyday life to get more manageable. They do **not** want a machine replacing the people they love. Good. Neither do I.

We are not here to become “an AI person.”

We are here to ask better questions than a blank search box.

## Safety — before anyone types

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Do not paste these into AI  
Social Security number  
Bank, card, or Medicare numbers  
Passwords or phone codes  
Someone else’s private business

Read that again.

If a website, a pop-up, or a stranger in a chat asks you to paste those things “so the AI can help,” that is not this lesson. That is Lesson 3 material. Close it.

You *may* paste a paragraph you want explained if you cover the name, the address, and the account number first. “Dear member, your appointment is March 4” is enough. The medical record number is not.

Health, money, and legal answers are **drafts**. Show them to a human who is qualified before you act.

## A weak question, then a real one

Watch what happens when I ask a lazy question.

`[SHOW]` Type slowly:

`dinners`

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Weak: “dinners”

That will get me *something*. It will not get me *my* life.

Now the real question. This is the pattern I want on your fridge.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Situation + Ask + Format

You tell it who you are, what you need, and what the answer should look like.

`[SHOW]` New chat. Type slowly, out loud:

> I am 68. I cook for two people. I have leftover roast chicken, rice, and fresh spinach in the fridge. Give me three easy dinners. No spicy food. No oven if I can help it. For each dinner, list any extra ingredients I still need and the steps in short sentences.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
One real question  
Who I am  
What I have  
What I want back

Send it.

`[PAUSE — let the answer appear]`

Look at what came back. Three dinners. A list. Steps.

Is it useful? That is the only test.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
A useful answer  
I understand it  
I could do the next step  
Nothing private was required

If you understand it, you could do the next step, and you did not have to hand over your life — that is a useful answer.

If one dinner sounds wrong — maybe you do not eat dairy — you do not start over. You talk back.

## The follow-up is the skill

`[SHOW]` Type:

> Make the second dinner even simpler. I only want one pan. I do not have cream. Use words a tired person can follow at 6 p.m.

Send.

`[PAUSE]`

That is prompting. People online make it sound like a profession. It is just manners plus detail. You already do this when you give directions to a neighbor.

Other follow-ups that work:

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Follow-ups that work  
“Make that simpler.”  
“Give me three options.”  
“I have 15 minutes. Cut this down.”  
“Explain the second step like I have never done it.”  
“Now write this as a short email I can copy.”

Try one of those on *your* question — the one from the sticky note in Lesson 1.

If you did not write a question, use this:

> I am an adult over 50 and I feel behind on AI. Give me three practical ways to use a tool like this this week. Do not ask for any personal numbers. Use plain English.

## How to judge the answer — and what to do when it is wrong

AI will sometimes invent a detail. Researchers and the companies themselves warn about this. I have seen a tool invent a clinic phone number that was not real.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
If it sounds too sure  
Check the part that matters  
A name, a number, a “always” or “never”

You do not need to check every adjective.

You check the thing you might act on.

- A recipe: do you actually have that ingredient? Is the cook time sane?
- A letter: does it sound like you, or like a lawyer in a movie?
- A health explanation: is it a starting point for your *own* clinician, or are you about to change a pill?
- A “best restaurant”: did it close in 2019? Look it up.

If the answer is mush, say so.

> That is too long. Give me five bullets.

or

> You made something up. I only want steps that use chicken, rice, and spinach.

You are not being rude. You are steering.

`[PAUSE]`

One more rule. Do not paste a long email from your bank or your doctor with the account number still in it. Cover the number. Ask: “Explain this in plain English. I blacked out the account.”

## What this is good for this week

I am going to name uses I see in our rooms. Steal one.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Good first jobs for AI  
Rewrite a letter so it is shorter  
Explain a paragraph you do not understand  
Make a packing list  
Practice interview answers  
Turn leftovers into a plan  
Draft a thank-you note  
Give you three questions to ask a doctor — then you ask the doctor

Notice what is not on the list.

It is not “let the tool decide my medication.”

It is not “paste my tax return.”

It is not “talk to a stranger who says they are tech support.”

Those are how people get hurt.

AARP’s own research says adults 50+ are most interested in translation, safety, fraud prevention, health *questions*, and learning a skill. That is a grown person’s list. We will honor it.

If you are still working, you are allowed to use this at the office the same way: one real task, no confidential files, no customer Social Security numbers. When in doubt, ask your supervisor what may leave the building.

## Common stalls — and the fix

**“I don’t know what to ask.”**  
Look at the sticky note. Or ask the tool to propose three questions for an adult your age who cooks / gardens / job-hunts / cares for a spouse.

**“The login scared me.”**  
Use the email you already have. Write the new password in the notebook. Come back. The win is on the other side of two minutes of annoyance.

**“It gave me too much.”**  
“Shorter. Five lines.”

**“I feel silly talking to a box.”**  
You are not talking to a friend. You are using a tool. You can close the tab when you are done. It will not be lonely.

**“My family said I would break it.”**  
You cannot break ChatGPT by asking about chicken. You can waste twenty minutes. That is the worst case. We can afford twenty minutes.

## Do this before you leave

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Before you go  
1. Save or screenshot the useful answer  
2. Try one follow-up  
3. Do not paste anything private  
4. Come to Lesson 3 still proud — then we lock the door

If you are in a live room: turn to a partner and read your question out loud before you type it. Hearing it usually makes it clearer.

If you are at home: do the question now, while the tab is open. Do not “save it for later.” Later is where good intentions go.
