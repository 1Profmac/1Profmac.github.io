## Skill 1 — Spot a fake

A fake wants you to believe the costume.

`[SHOW]` Obviously fake email graphic.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
From: amazon-security@email-secure-alert.com  
Subject: Your account will close in 20 minutes

Look at the costume, not the logo.

**The sender.** Would Amazon really write from `email-secure-alert.com`? Hover or tap and look. Do not click the link to “check.”

**The greeting.** “Dear Customer” from a bank that knows your name is a tell. Not always. Often.

**The ask.** A link to “verify.” A phone number in the message. A QR code you did not ask for. A download. A request for a code that just arrived on your phone.

**The surprise.** You did not start this conversation. That matters more than the grammar. Plenty of scams are now well written. AI made the spelling better. Do not use “no typos” as your safety test. That test is dead.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Spot a fake  
Who started this?  
Does the address look odd?  
Are they sending me *to* a link or number?  
Would my bank already know my name?

`[SHOW]` Fake text.

“Social Security will be suspended in 30 minutes. Call 1-888-555-0147 now.”

The real Social Security Administration does not suspend your number because you missed a text. The real IRS does not demand gift cards. The real FTC has to say this so often they printed it: they will **never** threaten you, tell you to move money to “protect it,” or send you to a Bitcoin ATM.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
The FTC will never  
Threaten you  
Tell you to move money to “protect it”  
Demand gift cards or crypto

If the costume is a bank, Medicare, Amazon, Microsoft, Apple, a sheriff, a grandchild, or “tech support” that called *you* — treat it as a costume until **you** started the call on a number you already had.

How do you check?

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Hang up. Look up. Call back.

Hang up.

Look up the number on a statement, the back of the card, or the official website you type yourself.

Call that number.

Do not call the number in the text. That number is the costume’s sleeve.

This is the Federal Trade Commission’s advice, not mine. I am just unwilling to dress it up.

## Skill 2 — Name the pressure

Fakes work because of feelings, not because you cannot read.

Learn the four levers. Say them out loud if you are in a room.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Pressure tactics  
Hurry  
Fear  
Secrecy  
Authority

**Hurry.** “Twenty minutes.” “A warrant.” “Your grandson is at the jail.” “The refund expires.” Hurry is the product. If you are not allowed to think, you are not in a real official process.

**Fear.** Arrest. Deportation. A virus on the computer. A stolen identity they will “help” you fix. Fear makes smart people obedient.

**Secrecy.** “Don’t tell your wife. She would worry.” “Don’t tell the bank teller what it’s for.” Isolation is the scam. A real emergency can stand a second adult.

**Authority.** A badge number. A case number. A familiar logo. Caller ID that says “Austin Police” or “IRS.” Caller ID is a costume. They can fake it.

There is a fifth lever I want you to memorize because the FTC keeps seeing it take retirement money.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Never move money to “protect it.”

Someone says your account is compromised. To keep the money safe, you must wire it, buy gold, feed a crypto ATM, or hand cash to a courier. That money is not going into a vault with your name on it. It is going to them.

Older adults have reported losses of tens and hundreds of thousands on that one story. Combined $100,000-plus impersonation losses rose eightfold from 2020 to 2024. That is the FTC’s own spotlight.

`[PAUSE]`

Payment methods that should make your hand stop:

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Stop if they want  
Gift cards  
Wire  
Crypto or a Bitcoin ATM  
A courier at the door  
Remote access to your computer

No legitimate tax office, bank, or grandchild-in-trouble story needs a Walmart gift card. I will die on that sentence.

**Tech support** deserves its own breath. A pop-up says your computer is infected. A polite person offers to “take over the screen.” Older adults are **five times** more likely than younger adults to report losing money on that scam, says the FTC. The FBI counted hundreds of millions more through its own system.

If you did not call them, they do not get to drive.

## The new costume — a voice you love

This is the part families asked us to add. You were right to ask.

AARP found roughly **eight or nine in ten** adults 50+ are worried about cloned voices and deepfake video. You are not imagining the pit in your stomach.

Microsoft, AARP, and the Better Business Bureau looked at more than half a million fraud reports. Scams people flagged as AI-enabled — realistic voices or videos — jumped **about twenty times** from 2023 to 2025.

Someone can call and sound like a grandchild. A few seconds of old video or voicemail can be enough.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
A familiar voice  
is no longer proof

So we use a 20th-century fix for a 21st-century trick.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Family code word  
Known only to your people  
Used when money or panic shows up  
If they don’t know it — hang up

Pick a word or short phrase you would never put on Facebook. “Blue canoe.” The name of a dog that died in 1998. Share it this week with the humans you would actually send money to.

If a panicked voice asks for bail, tickets, or a gift card and cannot give the word — you hang up. Then you call *your* person on the number in your phone.

You are not cruel for checking. You are literate.

AARP still sees more impostors posing as **companies and agencies** than as family. So do not only guard the grandchild story. Guard the “Amazon + bank + sheriff” pile-up too. They will transfer you from one costume to the next in a single call.

## Skill 3 — Passwords and the second lock

A password is a key. Most people have one key and twenty doors.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology — NIST, the people who write the grown-up rules — changed the advice a lot of us grew up with.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
NIST, in plain English  
Longer is better — aim for 15+ characters  
A phrase beats P@ssw0rd!  
You do not have to change it every 90 days  
Turn on a second lock

A passphrase: `maple-porch-radio-Thursday` is stronger and kinder to your memory than `Mg#9!x`.

If a site still demands a capital and a symbol, add them. Fine. Length is still the hero.

**Unique** matters. The password on email cannot be the password on the bank. Email is the skeleton key. If they get email, they can reset everything else.

What if you cannot remember twenty phrases?

Two honest options:

1. A **password manager** — a vault app — if you have someone who can sit with you once to set it up. Protect *that* vault with a long phrase and a second lock.
2. A **small notebook** in a drawer or a lockbox at home. Not a sticky note on the monitor. Not a photo in an unlocked camera roll. This is not shameful. Researchers who sat with older adults heard, “I’m 73, you can’t expect me to have multiple passwords.” A hidden notebook beats one reused password on the internet.

`[PAUSE]`

Now the second lock.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
2FA  
Two-factor authentication  
Password + something you have

After the password, the site sends a code to your phone, or asks an app, or uses your face on a device you already unlocked. A thief with only the password should bounce.

CISA — the U.S. cyber defense agency — says any second factor is better than none. An authenticator app or a passkey is stronger than a text message. Text messages can be stolen. Still: **turn it on** for email and banking this week. Perfect is the enemy of a locked door.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Turn it on this week  
Email first  
Then the bank or credit union

Where: Settings → Security → Two-factor, 2-step verification, or Multifactor. The words change. The idea does not.

**The hole in the lesson if I stop here:**

`[ON SCREEN]`  
Never read a code to anyone

The Federal Trade Commission is blunt. The code that just arrived is a second key. Anyone who calls or texts and asks you to read it to them is a scammer — even if they already know your name, even if they say they are the bank, even if they are “helping you stop a hack.”

You type the code into the site **you** opened. You never speak it to a stranger.

If you want the next step after 2FA, look up **passkeys** on the accounts that offer them. Face or fingerprint on your device, no code to steal. Optional. Not required for today.

## If it already happened

Some of you are not here for prevention. You are here because something already left the account.

Listen to me.

This is not a moral failure. Professionals ran a play on a human nervous system. You are allowed to still be a competent person.

`[ON SCREEN]`  
If money already moved  
1. Stop sending  
2. Call the bank — number on the card  
3. Say wire / gift card / crypto ATM if that is what you used  
4. Change email password. Turn on 2FA  
5. Report: ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov  
6. Tell one trusted person

Speed matters. The FTC works with the FBI’s recovery team. Recovery is not a promise. Silence is a guarantee of no chance.

If you gave a password or a code, change it from a device you trust. If a stranger had remote access, get a second pair of eyes — a library digital-help hour, a credit union, a family member who will not shame you.

Identity theft steps live at identitytheft.gov.
