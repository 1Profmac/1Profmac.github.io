## Page 2 — Talk to AI cheat sheet

**Pick one tool.** ChatGPT (chatgpt.com, free) or Copilot if it is already on your Windows PC.

**Never paste:** Social Security number · bank or Medicare numbers · passwords · phone codes · someone else’s private information.

**The pattern**

> I am [who]. I need [this]. Give me [this shape]. Use simple English.

**Follow-ups**

- Make that simpler.
- Give me three options.
- I have 15 minutes. Cut this down.
- Explain the second step like I have never done it.

**A useful answer:** I understand it. I could do the next step. I did not have to hand over private numbers.

**If it sounds too sure:** check the name, the number, or any “always / never” before you act. Health, money, and the law need a qualified human.

## Page 3 — Don’t get scammed cheat sheet

### Spot a fake

- Who started this conversation — them or me?
- Does the real email address look odd?
- Are they sending me to a link, a QR code, or a number *they* provided?
- Would my bank or Medicare already know my name?

**Hang up. Look up. Call back** on the number on the statement or the back of the card.

The real FTC will never threaten you, tell you to move money to “protect it,” or demand gift cards or crypto.

### Pressure tactics

| Lever | What it sounds like | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hurry | “Twenty minutes or you are arrested.” | You are allowed to think. Hang up. |
| Fear | Virus, warrant, grandchild in jail. | Call *your* person on *your* number. |
| Secrecy | “Don’t tell your spouse or the teller.” | Isolation is the scam. Tell someone. |
| Authority | Badge, caller ID, case number. | Costumes. Verify separately. |
| “Protect it” | Wire, gold, crypto ATM, courier. | Never move money to protect it. |

**Stop if they want:** gift cards · wire · crypto / Bitcoin ATM · a courier · remote control of your computer · the code that just arrived on your phone.

### Family code word

Word or phrase (not posted online): ________________________

People who know it: ______________________________________

If a panicked voice wants money and does not know the word — hang up. Then call the number in your phone.

A familiar voice is no longer proof.

### Passwords and 2FA

- Longer is better. Aim for **15+ characters**. A phrase beats `P@ssw0rd!`.
- Email password ≠ bank password. Email is the skeleton key.
- Notebook in a drawer or a lockbox is allowed. Sticky note on the monitor is not.
- Turn on **2FA** (two-factor / 2-step / multifactor) on **email first**, then the bank.
- **Never read a verification code to anyone.** You type it into the site you opened.

Settings → Security → Two-factor, 2-step verification, or Multifactor.

### If money already moved

1. Stop sending more.
2. Call the bank or card — number on the card or statement.
3. Say if you used a wire, gift card, or crypto ATM.
4. Change the email password. Turn on 2FA.
5. Report: **reportfraud.ftc.gov** and **ic3.gov**
6. Tell one trusted person. If identity was stolen: **identitytheft.gov**

## Practice (optional, five minutes)

**Which of these is safest?**

1. A text: “Your Social Security will be suspended in 30 minutes. Call this number.”
2. You call Social Security using the number on ssa.gov or your statement.
3. A pop-up: “Your PC is infected. Click here so Microsoft can help.”

Answer: 2.

**Which prompt is stronger?**

A. “email”  
B. “I need a short, polite email to my HOA about a recurring parking problem. Five sentences. No anger. I am a resident, not a lawyer.”

Answer: B.

**True or false:** If the caller ID says your bank, it is your bank.

Answer: False. Caller ID is a costume.

**True or false:** Anyone who asks you to read the code that just arrived on your phone is helping you.

Answer: False. The FTC says that person is a scammer.
