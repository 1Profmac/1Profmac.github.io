## Lesson 1 — Welcome: you are not too late

### Adults 50+ already use technology

AARP’s 2025–2026 technology research is the cleanest public picture of this audience.

- **90%** of adults 50+ own a smartphone. Ownership rose from 55% in 2016.
- Adults 50+ own an average of **seven** devices and use them daily.
- **99%** own at least one common tech device.
- Texting is now the lead communication method in this age group.
- **9 in 10** use social media. **8 in 10** stream video weekly.

Source: AARP, *Research Insights on Technology* and *Tech Use and Adoption Growing Among Adults Age 50-Plus* (2026).  
https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/aarp-research-insights-technology/  
https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/

**How to say it:** “You are not starting from zero. Most adults over 50 already have a smartphone in their pocket. What you have not had is a teacher who respects your intelligence.”

### Generative AI use is rising fast — and still early

AARP tracked generative AI use among adults 50+:

| Year | Share of adults 50+ who have used generative AI |
| --- | --- |
| 2023 | 9% |
| 2024 | 18% |
| 2025 | 30% |

That is a near-tripling in two years, and a near-doubling from 2024 to 2025.

More nuance from AARP’s *Navigating the World of AI* data story:

- **8 in 10** adults 50+ have used *some* form of AI (predictive text, voice assistants, and the like).
- **42%** call themselves beginners. **28%** intermediate. **7%** advanced. **1%** expert. **23%** say they have never used AI.
- **65%** believe AI could help with daily activities and independence.
- Only **51%** believe the benefits outweigh the risks.
- Employed adults 50+ are twice as likely to have used generative AI (**42%**) as those not employed (**21%**).

Sources:  
https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/aarp-research-insights-technology/  
https://datastories.aarp.org/2026/navigating-the-world-of-ai/  
AARP PDF: *Navigating the World of AI: Attitudes, Awareness, and Openness Among Adults Ages 50-Plus*  
https://www.aarp.org/content/dam/aarp/research/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/artificial-intelligence-survey.doi.10.26419-2fres.00888.001.pdf

**How to say it:** “In one year, generative AI use among people our age nearly doubled — from 18 percent to 30 percent. That is not a fad. That is a door opening. Most people who walk through it still call themselves beginners. Beginner is a fine place to be.”

### Why people freeze

Internal 50+TechBridge listening (August 2026) matched public research. The five pain phrases that showed up everywhere:

1. “Is it too late for me?”
2. “Nobody explains it at my level.”
3. “I feel stupid when I try.”
4. “My family doesn’t have time to help me.”
5. “I’m afraid of being scammed / doing something wrong.”

Supporting public signal: AARP finds **3 in 5** adults 50+ say technology is not designed with their age in mind. ConsumerAffairs (2026) found **38%** of Boomers and Gen X feel tech-induced overwhelm daily.

**How to say it:** “The problem was never your brain. The problem was the teacher, the pace, and the shame.”

### Teaching method that works

Peer-reviewed work on AI literacy for older adults (2025) found people prefer **hands-on exploration** over lectures, and they learn faster when the task is a real-life scenario, not a definition. A 2026 program built on geragogy and Kolb’s experiential learning raised **self-efficacy and attitudes toward AI**. First successful use is the product. Theory can wait.

Source: *AI Literacy Education for Older Adults: Motivations, Challenges and Preferences*, arXiv:2504.14649.  
https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14649v1

**How we designed Lesson 2:** one tool, one real question, one useful answer. Not a tour of ten apps.

## Lesson 2 — Talk to AI: one question, one useful answer

### What “useful” means

AARP finds older adults want AI to make everyday life more manageable — translation, home safety, fraud prevention, health questions, skill-building — **without replacing human contact**. Practical beats clever.

**Prompt pattern we teach** (plain English, not “prompt engineering”):

> Situation + Ask + Format  
> “I am [who]. I need [this]. Give me [this shape]. Use simple English.”

Then one follow-up: “Make that simpler” or “Give me three options.”

### Safety rules before the first prompt

These are not optional. Put them on screen before anyone types.

Never paste into an AI tool:

- Social Security number
- Bank, credit card, or Medicare numbers
- Passwords or 2FA codes
- Full medical record numbers
- Someone else’s private information

AI can be **confidently wrong**. Treat health, money, and legal answers as a draft. Verify with a person who is qualified.

This is consistent with the U.S. Department of Labor’s AI literacy framing: people should know how to **direct** AI, **question** AI, and keep **human judgment**. We do not name the DOL framework on camera unless the audience is a workforce board.

## Lesson 3 — Don't get scammed

### The scale — FTC (Consumer Sentinel)

FTC report to Congress, *Protecting Older Consumers 2024–2025*, released December 1, 2025:

- Adults **60 and older** reported **$2.4 billion** in fraud losses in 2024.
- That is up from about **$600 million** in 2020 — a **300%** increase, about **fourfold**.
- Reports of any dollar loss rose only **59%**. The explosion is in **very large** losses.
- Losses over **$100,000** were **5%** of older adults’ loss reports and **68%** of the dollars.
- Older adults were **nearly twice as likely** as younger adults to report a six-figure loss.
- Median reported loss, ages 60+: **$900** (up from $650 in 2023).
- Median reported loss, ages 80+: **$1,650**.
- Highest median loss by contact method: **phone, $2,210**. Social media: **$650**.
- Aggregate dollars and number of loss reports were **highest for social media**.
- Social-media-started losses by older adults rose **nearly ninefold** since 2020. Text-scam losses rose **more than eightfold**.
- People 80+ still lose the most, and report the most, on **phone** scams.
- Older adults were **five times** more likely than younger adults to report losing money on a **tech support** scam.
- FTC-reported tech support losses by older adults: **$159 million** in 2024.
- Top dollar categories: **investment**, **business impersonation**, **government impersonation**.
- About **74%** of older adults’ fraud reports showed **no money lost**. They spotted it.
- Because of underreporting, FTC staff estimate true 2024 cost to older adults at **$10.1 billion to $81.5 billion**.

Sources:  
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-issues-annual-report-congress-agencys-actions-protect-older-adults  
PDF: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P144400-OlderAdultsReportDec2025.pdf  
August 2025 impersonation spotlight:  
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-data-show-more-four-fold-increase-reports-impersonation-scammers-stealing-tens-even-hundreds

**How to say it:** “The Federal Trade Commission says adults 60 and older reported $2.4 billion stolen in 2024 — four times what they reported in 2020. The jump is not that more people are ‘bad at computers.’ The jump is that a smaller number of scams are taking life-changing amounts of money. Phone calls still take the most per person. And here is the part they leave out of the scare stories: three out of four older adults who reported a scam lost nothing. They spotted it. That can be you.”

### The scale — FBI IC3

FBI *2024 Internet Crime Report*:

- People over 60 suffered the most losses of any age group: **nearly $5 billion**.
- They also submitted the most complaints.
- AARP’s summary of the same report: **$4.9 billion**, **43%** higher than the year before, more than **147,000** complaints, average loss about **$83,000**.
- Investment scams were the costliest category for victims 60+ (**about $1.8 billion**).
- Tech support: **$982 million**.
- Confidence / romance: **about $389 million**.
- All ages: **859,532** complaints, **$16.6 billion** in reported losses, up **33%**.

Sources:  
https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-annual-internet-crime-report  
https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2024_IC3Report.pdf  
https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/fbi-report-fraud-2024/

**How to say it:** “The FBI’s internet crime center tells the same story with a different stack of reports: people over 60 lost nearly five billion dollars in 2024. Investment schemes took the most money. Fake ‘tech support’ took almost a billion.”

### Impersonation and pressure

FTC, August 2025: reports from older adults who lost **$10,000 or more** to impersonators (government or business) rose more than **fourfold** since 2020. Combined losses in the **$100,000+** impersonation cases rose **eightfold**, from **$55 million** (2020) to **$445 million** (2024).

The script: unexpected problem + urgency + “move your money to keep it safe.” The money goes to the scammer.

FTC consumer rules we teach word for word:

- Never transfer or send money, cryptocurrency, or gold to someone you do not know after an unexpected call or message.
- Do not move money to “protect it.”
- Hang up. Call the company or agency on a number **you** look up — statement, back of the card, official website. Not the number they gave you.
- Government and the real FTC will never threaten you, demand gift cards, or tell you to put cash in a Bitcoin ATM.

https://consumer.ftc.gov/features/how-avoid-imposter-scams  
https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-government-impersonation-scam  
https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-data-show-more-four-fold-increase-reports-impersonation-scammers-stealing-tens-even-hundreds

### AI-enabled scams and voice cloning

AARP, August 2024 survey of 1,000 adults 50+ (NORC Foresight 50+):

- Password cracking: **87%** concerned
- Deepfakes: **85%**
- Spear phishing: **84%**
- Voice cloning: **84%**
- **77%** worry they may personally be a target of AI-related fraud

https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/work-finances-retirement/fraud-consumer-protection/ai-fraud-concerns-older-adults/

AARP, June 2025 survey of 1,888 adults 50+:

- **95%** received a scam or illegal robocall in the past year
- **39%** say it happens daily or almost daily
- **84%** concerned about AI-generated fake voices (**51%** very concerned)

https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/work-finances-retirement/fraud-consumer-protection/ai-robocalls-congressional-legislation/

Microsoft AI for Good Lab, with AARP Fraud Watch Network and BBB Scam Tracker (reported by AARP, December 2025):

- Analyzed **531,000** fraud reports.
- Scams victims identified as **AI-enabled** (realistic voices or videos) increased **20-fold** from 2023 to 2025.
- Half of scams reported to AARP’s helpline in 2025 were **impostor** schemes. Most posed as businesses or officials, not family.

https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/detecting-ai-fraud/

**How to say it:** “Nine out of ten people our age are already worried about fake voices and fake videos. You are not imagining it. Microsoft, AARP, and the Better Business Bureau looked at more than half a million reports. Scams people flagged as AI-powered jumped twenty times in two years. The answer is not panic. The answer is a rule: if the call is strange, hang up and check.”

### Family code word

AARP and independent fraud experts now recommend a **family safe word** for unexpected money or emergency calls. The cloned voice can sound like your grandchild. It does not know the word you agreed on last Sunday.

https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/detecting-ai-fraud/

**How to say it:** “Pick a word your family would never put on Facebook. Share it with the people you would actually send money to. If a panicked voice asks for cash and does not know the word — hang up.”

### Passwords — NIST, not folklore

NIST’s current consumer guidance (*How Do I Create a Good Password?*):

- Prefer **multifactor authentication**. Passkeys are a strong option.
- Use a **password manager** when you can.
- If you must invent a password, make it **at least 15 characters**. A passphrase is easier to remember than a short “P@ssw0rd!”.
- NIST **no longer** tells people to rotate passwords on a schedule or stuff them with odd symbols as the main defense. **Length** matters more than a circus of characters.
- Many sites still demand a symbol. Fine. Add one. Just do not make “Tr0ubadour!” your whole strategy.

https://www.nist.gov/cybersecurity-and-privacy/how-do-i-create-a-good-password

Research with older adults (2025) found many cannot or will not juggle dozens of digital vaults. A **written password book kept in a safe place at home** is an acceptable bridge. Shame is a worse security practice than a notebook in a drawer.

### 2FA / MFA — CISA and FTC

CISA: any MFA is better than a password alone. Authenticator apps and passkeys beat text-message codes. Text codes can be stolen by SIM-swap. Still: **turn it on**.

https://www.cisa.gov/MFA

FTC: a verification code is a second key to your account. **Anyone who asks you to read them that code is a scammer.** Hang up. Report it.

https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/03/whats-verification-code-why-would-someone-ask-me-it

**How to say it:** “Two-factor authentication means your password is not enough. The second lock is usually a code on your phone. That code is only for you. If a polite stranger asks you to read it to them, the conversation is over.”

### If money already moved

Teach this so Lesson 3 does not end on dread:

1. Stop. Do not send more.
2. Call the bank, credit union, or card company on the number **on the card or statement**.
3. If you wired money or used a crypto ATM, say so immediately. Speed matters.
4. Change passwords on email first, then money accounts. Turn on 2FA.
5. Report at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov) and [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov).
6. Tell one trusted person. Shame is what scammers count on.

The FTC partners with FBI IC3’s Recovery Asset Team. Recovery is not guaranteed. Fast reporting is the only chance.
